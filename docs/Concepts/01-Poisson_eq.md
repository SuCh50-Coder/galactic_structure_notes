# **Poisson Equation**

??? note

    The following is an excerpt of the derivation from the famous book [**Galactic Dynamics**](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691130279/galactic-dynamics) by Binney & Tremaine.  

## Deriving the differential form

Our goal is to calculate the force $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})$ on a particle of mass $m_s$ at position $\mathbf{x}$ that is generated by the gravitational attraction of a distribution of mass $\rho(\mathbf{x}')$. According to Newton’s inverse-square law of gravitation, the force $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})$ may be obtained by summing the small contributions

\begin{equation}
\delta\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = G m_s \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \delta m(\mathbf{x}') = G m_s \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \rho(\mathbf{x}') \mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}'
\label{eq:delta_force}
\end{equation}

to the overall force from each small element of volume $\mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}'$ located at $\mathbf{x}'$. Thus

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = m_s \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{where} \quad \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) \equiv G \int \mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}' \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \rho(\mathbf{x}')
\label{eq:grav_field}
\end{equation}

is the **gravitational field**, the force per unit mass.

If we define the **gravitational potential** $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ by

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Phi(\mathbf{x}) \equiv -G \int \mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}' \frac{\rho(\mathbf{x}')}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|},}
\label{eq:phi_def}
\end{equation}

and notice that

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}} \left( \frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|} \right) = \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3},
\end{equation}

we find that we may write $\mathbf{g}$ as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) &= \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}} \int \mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}' \frac{G\rho(\mathbf{x}')}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|} \\
&= -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:grad_phi}
\end{equation}

where for brevity we have dropped the subscript $\mathbf{x}$ on the gradient operator $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$.

The potential is useful because it is a scalar field that is easier to visualize than the vector gravitational field but contains the same information. Also, in many situations the easiest way to obtain $\mathbf{g}$ is first to calculate the potential and then to take its gradient.

If we take the divergence of equation $\ref{eq:grav_field}$, we find

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) = G \int \mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}' \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \right) \rho(\mathbf{x}').
\label{eq:divg}
\end{equation}

Now

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \right) = -\frac{3}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} + \frac{3(\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}) \cdot (\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^5}.
\end{equation}

When $\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x} \neq 0$ we may cancel the factor $|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^2$ from top and bottom of the last term in this equation to conclude that

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \right) = 0 \quad (\mathbf{x}' \neq \mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}

Therefore, any contribution to the integral of equation $\ref{eq:divg}$ must come from the point $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x}$, and we may restrict the volume of integration to a small sphere of radius $h$ centered on this point. Since, for sufficiently small $h$, the density will be almost constant through this volume, we can take $\rho(\mathbf{x}')$ out of the integral. The remaining terms of the integrand may then be arranged as follows:



\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) &= G\rho(\mathbf{x}) \int_{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}| \le h} \mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}' \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \right) \nonumber \\
&= -G\rho(\mathbf{x}) \int_{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}| \le h} \mathrm{d}^3\mathbf{x}' \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}'} \cdot \left( \frac{\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3} \right) \label{eq:div_seq_a} \\
&= -G\rho(\mathbf{x}) \int_{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}| = h} \mathrm{d}^2\mathbf{S}' \cdot \frac{(\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}|^3}. \nonumber
\end{align}


The last step in this sequence uses the divergence theorem to convert the volume integral into a surface integral.  
Now on the sphere $|\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}| = h$ we have $\mathrm{d}^2\mathbf{S}' = (\mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}) h \mathrm{d}^2\Omega$, where $\mathrm{d}^2\Omega$ is a small element of solid angle. Hence equation $\ref{eq:div_seq_a}$ becomes

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) = -G\rho(\mathbf{x}) \int \mathrm{d}^2\Omega = -4\pi G\rho(\mathbf{x}).
\label{eq:div_seq_b}
\end{equation}

!!! success "Poisson Equation"

    If we substitute from equation ($\ref{eq:grad_phi}$) for $\boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{g}$, we obtain **Poisson’s equation** relating the potential $\Phi$ to the density $\rho$:

    \begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho.
    \label{eq:poisson}
    \end{equation}

This is a differential equation that can be solved for $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ given $\rho(\mathbf{x})$ and an appropriate boundary condition.[^1] For an isolated system the boundary condition is $\Phi \to 0$ as $|\mathbf{x}| \to \infty$. The potential given by equation ($\ref{eq:phi_def}$) automatically satisfies this boundary condition. 

[^1]: Using the physically correct boundary condition is essential: for example, if $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ is a solution, then so is $\Phi(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}$, with $\mathbf{k}$ an arbitrary constant vector, but the corresponding gravitational fields differ by $\mathbf{k}$.


## In Cylindrical Coordinates

Galactic systems have cylindrical symmetry. So, it is useful for us if we rewrite Eq. ($\ref{eq:poisson}$) in cylindrical coordinates. We shall assume the galactic potential to be _axisymmetric_ (i.e $\partial_{\phi} \Phi = 0$). Hence both the mass density and galactic potential must depend only on the _radial_ ($R$) and _vertical_ ($z$) coordinates.

\begin{align}
 \boldsymbol{\nabla}^2\Phi (R,z) &= 4\pi G\rho (R,z) \\
\frac{1}{R} \frac{\partial}{\partial R} \left( R \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial R} \right) + \frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial z^2} &= 4\pi G\rho (R,z) \\
\end{align}

!!! note "Force terms"

    Define the gravitational force per unit mass in the radial direction as

    $$ F_R \equiv - \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial R},$$

    and in the vertical direction as

    $$ K_z \equiv - \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial z}.$$


also make an assumption that the system is symmetric about $z = 0$ plane. So, we can write the **total surface mass density $\Sigma (R,z)$** as,

\[ \Sigma (R,z) = \int_{-z}^{+z} \rho (R,z') \dd z = 2 \int_{0}^{+z} \rho (R,z') \dd z' \]

Using these definitions, we can integrate the above equation as, 

\begin{equation}
\int_{0}^{+z}  \dd z' \frac{1}{R} \pdv{}{R} (R F_R) + \int_{0}^{+z}  \dd z' \pdv{K_z}{z} = - 2 \pi G \times 2 \int_{0}^{+z}  \dd z' \rho (R,z') 
\end{equation}

to obtain, 

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
-2\pi G \, \Sigma(R, z) = K_z(R, z) + \int_{0}^{+z} \frac{1}{R} \frac{\partial \big(R F_R \big)}{\partial R} \, dz} 
\end{equation}

This is known as the integrated form of Poissonian Equation, for an axisymmetric galactic disc.
